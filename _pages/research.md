My current research sits at the intersection of AI, management, and decision sciences. I pursue two complementary streams, united by a broader agenda: building computationally rigorous, decision-relevant, and scientifically grounded tools in **Human + AI + Decision Science**.

## Current Research

<div class="research-stream">
<div class="research-stream-header">
<span class="research-stream-label">Research Stream 1</span><span class="research-stream-title">Decision-Aware Learning</span>
</div>
<p class="research-stream-intro">I study how firms can design learning systems that optimize not only predictive accuracy, but also the quality of the downstream decisions they support.</p>
<details class="research-stream-papers">
<summary><span class="wp-title">Decision-Aware Segmentation</span><span class="wp-badge">Working Paper</span></summary>
<div class="working-paper">
<p class="wp-authors">with Sandeep Chandukala (SMU), Ernst Osinga (SMU), and Spyros Zoumpoulis (INSEAD)</p>
<p class="wp-abstract">Customer segmentation is everywhere in marketing. However, widely used segmentation methods are descriptive rather than prescriptive: they partition customers by optimizing statistical criteria (e.g., minimizing within-cluster distances),
without regard to the firm’s downstream targeting objective. As a result, segments that are statistically well-formed may induce suboptimal targeting policies.This paper asks a fundamental question: if the goal of segmentation is to decide who should receive which action, can we learn the segments with that decision in mind? We develop <strong>Decision-Aware Segmentation (DAS)</strong>, a framework that learns customer segments based on the value of the targeting policies they induce. More broadly, the paper positions segmentation as part of the decision problem itself, offering a middle ground between traditional descriptive clustering and fully individualized targeting.</p>
</div>
</details>

<div class="research-stream">
<div class="research-stream-header">
<span class="research-stream-label">Research Stream 2</span><span class="research-stream-title">Human–AI Collaboration in Science</span>
</div>
<p class="research-stream-intro">I study how human researchers and AI systems can collaborate productively in the broader scientific process.</p>
<details class="research-stream-papers">
<summary><span class="wp-title">AI Theory Building</span><span class="wp-badge">Working Paper</span></summary>
<div class="working-paper">
<p class="wp-authors">with Philip Parker (INSEAD), Phanish Puranam (INSEAD), Eric Luis Uhlmann (INSEAD), and Spyros Zoumpoulis (INSEAD)</p>
<div class="wp-abstract">
<p class="wp-abstract">Generative AI is moving rapidly into tasks that sit close to the intellectual core of science. This paper asks a broader question: what are the comparative advantages of generative AI and human scientists in scientific theory building? We compare human scientists and generative AI systems as they predict previously unseen empirical patterns in large-scale data on scholarly discourse concerning racial and gender inequality, construct theoretical explanations and causal models, and revise those theories after observing machine-learning evidence. Our results point to distinct strengths on each side. Generative AI already matches or exceeds the average human scientist across several theory-building tasks and performs especially well when reasoning about complex interactions. Its theories are also more elaborate, containing more causal paths and latent variables. Human scientists, however, produce a much wider range of ideas, and this diversity generates larger gains when their judgments are aggregated. Together, these findings suggest a future of scientific discovery in which AI contributes scale and complexity, while humans remain an important source of intellectual diversity and collective intelligence.</p>
</div>
